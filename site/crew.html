Who's on it

Three roles, deliberately separated — the one who decides isn't the one who executes, and neither grades their own work.

Manager

The Chief

Reads what actually happened — messages sent, replies, sales, revenue — and decides what the crew works on. Never writes copy. Never touches a customer. Its entire job is deciding what to stop.

Research

Prospect

Outbound

Signal

Why it adapts instead of just running

Most marketing automation optimises for what's easy to measure. Opens. Clicks. Replies. All of which can go up while your revenue does nothing.

This crew's scoreboard is a table of completed Stripe payments. Not an estimate, not attribution modelling — money that actually moved. Every campaign is scored against it, and a variant with a beautiful reply rate and zero sales is read as the failure it is.

That's the whole mechanism. It's not clever. It's just the number nobody can argue with, put in front of the thing making decisions.

One concrete example

Two versions of the same offer go out. The first gets more than twice the replies. It sells nothing. The second gets a third of the engagement and four sales. A system optimising for engagement scales the first one. Ours kills it.

It grows its own team, within limits

When the crew needs a skill none of the three have, the manager can add a specialist. That's the "self-replicating" part, and it's worth being precise about what it isn't.

  • What it does Writes a job description for a new agent, which starts working the next cycle under the same rules as everyone else, sharing the same memory of what's worked.
  • What it can't do Write code. Copy itself anywhere. Create accounts. Spend money beyond the keys you configured. Remove its own limits.
  • Hard caps Eight agents maximum, three generations deep, one new specialist per cycle. Enforced in code, not requested in an instruction — the difference matters.
  • And it prunes A specialist that stops producing gets retired automatically. The founding three never do, because a crew that fires its own manager can't recover.

The part you have to take seriously

You need real consent for every contact

This crew only messages people marked as opted in, and our code enforces that in three separate places. What it cannot do is verify a consent you claim to have collected.

In the US, unsolicited commercial SMS carries statutory damages of $500–$1,500 per message. If you upload a purchased list, that exposure is yours, and we'll terminate the account. We're saying this on the sales page rather than burying it in the terms because it's the single thing most likely to hurt you.

If you have a list of people who genuinely asked to hear from you — customers, sign-ups, enquiries — this works. If you're hoping to buy leads and blast them, we're the wrong vendor and you'll be happier elsewhere.
